Abstract

We present Forward, a three-person forward-deployed engineering unit for applied AI. Prior approaches to enterprise AI adoption rely predominantly on slide decks, steering committees and an eighteen-month roadmap [4], and have been observed to produce remarkably few systems that run. We propose an alternative. Senior engineers embed inside the client organisation, identify the small number of workflows where language models have measurable leverage, and build those workflows into production with evaluation suites, permission boundaries, observability and human review. We report a typical time to production of eight weeks (Section 5), a code handover rate of 100 %, and zero slide decks. We further show that the method is robust to the case where AI is the wrong tool, in which case we say so (Section 6). All code is delivered to the client with documentation. No lock-in mechanism was used in this work.

Keywords: forward-deployed engineering · agents · retrieval-augmented generation · evaluation · human-in-the-loop · GDPR · Sweden

Introduction

The gap between an AI demonstration and an AI system in production is well documented [1], [3] and, in our experience, consists almost entirely of things that are not the model: the permissions, the data that lives in the ERP, the eleven edge cases in the mail flow, and the person whose job it is to check. A demonstration can ignore all four. A system cannot.

Most efforts to close this gap begin by inserting a layer between the people who know the workflow and the people who can build software. The layer produces documents. The documents describe a future system, its governance, and a roadmap towards it, at a level of abstraction where nothing can fail because nothing has been attempted. We observe that this layer is expensive, and that it does not compile.

We remove the layer. Our contribution is not a model, a framework, or a platform. It is three senior engineers who sit inside the client, read the real data on day one, and are measured on whether software runs. Specifically:

(i) we define forward-deployed engineering for AI and its non-goals (Section 2); (ii) we describe a method with four components: mapping, build & integrate, private inference and enablement (Section 3); (iii) we describe the engagement protocol, which fits in one figure (Section 4); (iv) we report results (Section 5), followed by limitations that are genuinely limiting (Section 6) and an ethics statement written by people who have read the GDPR (Section 7).

The remainder of this document is the homepage.

Background: Forward-Deployed Engineering

A forward-deployed engineer is a senior software engineer who works physically or organisationally inside the client, holds read access to real data from the first week, and is evaluated on whether a system is in use rather than on whether a deliverable was delivered. The term originates in defence and large-scale data platform companies [2]; we apply it to a smaller, more Swedish setting.2

Concretely, FDE for AI means (a) sitting with the people who do the work; (b) instrumenting the workflow rather than interviewing about it; (c) building against real data under real permissions, not a sanitised sample; and (d) leaving behind the code, the evaluation suite and the runbook, such that the client's own engineers can operate the system without us. Property (d) distinguishes the method from the majority of vendor engagements, whose economics depend on its absence.

Non-goals

We do not produce strategy. We do not select vendors on the client's behalf, although we have opinions and will share them at no charge. We do not fine-tune a model when a prompt and a retrieval index would do, and we do not deploy a retrieval index when a SQL query would do [7]. We do not use the word transformation.

Working definition of "pays off"

We consider a workflow a candidate only if the expected value of automating or assisting it is positive after accounting for adoption and the friction of integration. For a set of candidate workflows W, we estimate

V = Σw ∈ W ℓ(w) · a(w) − F
(1)

where ℓ(w) is leverage (hours or errors removed per week), a(w) ∈ [0, 1] is realised adoption, and F is the integration friction, which is large, always underestimated, and paid by whoever builds the thing. Equation (1) is not sophisticated. It is, however, evaluated on real numbers, which we have found to be the differentiating factor.

Method

The method comprises four components, summarised in Table 1. They are sequential in the common case but may be invoked independently; in particular, Section 3.3 is frequently requested on its own by organisations whose data is not permitted to leave the building.

Workflow mapping

We spend the first one to two weeks inside the operation, observing and instrumenting rather than interviewing. Interviews report the process as designed; logs report the process as performed; the difference is where the leverage is. The output is a short ranked list and a recommendation. In roughly one engagement in three the recommendation for the top-ranked workflow is that a language model is the wrong tool, and that a form, a rule, or a conversation with the supplier would be cheaper. We consider this output a success and bill it as one.

Build & integrate

A single workflow is selected and built to production standard against the client's real systems. Figure 3 shows the reference architecture. The model is the smallest box in it, by design. The surrounding components—retrieval over the client's documents, tool access to line-of-business systems under the caller's permissions, an evaluation suite run on every change, guardrails on inputs and outputs, tracing, and an explicit human review step for consequential actions—are where the engineering time goes and where systems fail when they are skipped.

Every deployment ships with an evaluation suite constructed from the client's own historical data (Listing 1). The suite is run on every prompt, model or retrieval change and gates the release. We have not found a way to make this step optional that does not eventually produce an incident, and we have stopped looking.

Private inference

For workflows over data that cannot leave the organisation—health, legal, HR, anything a DPO looks at twice—we serve open-weight models on the client's own hardware or on infrastructure resident in the EU, behind the same evaluation suite as Section 3.2. Quality is lower than the frontier and, for the majority of enterprise workflows we have measured, still sufficient; the evaluation suite is how one finds out rather than argues. Purpose-built hardware for local inference is future work (Section 8).

Enablement

The system is handed over to the client's engineers with documentation, runbooks and a pairing period. We define handover as complete when a change to the system has been shipped to production by the client without our involvement. We remain on call afterwards. In our data the shared channel goes quiet within four to six weeks, which we interpret as the intended outcome rather than a churn event.

Engagement Protocol

The protocol has four phases and is shown in Figure 2. It has been designed so that the first thing the client sees is software, and so that the last thing the client sees is their own engineers deploying it.

Time to production is the sum of the first three phases,

Tprod = Tembed + Tproto + Tharden ≈ 0 + 2 + 6
(2)

weeks, where the embed term is approximately zero because we arrive with laptops and questions rather than a discovery phase. The hardening term dominates, which is correct: it is the part that determines whether the system is still running in a year.

During hardening the prototype is rebuilt rather than patched. The prototype exists to establish that the workflow is tractable and to produce the first evaluation set; it is not the product. We state this at the start of every engagement and it is believed at the end of every engagement.

Results

Figure 1 compares time to production for the proposed method against the prevailing approach, which we denote slide-deck consultancy. The comparison is illustrative. A rigorous version would require a consultancy to consent to being measured, and none has.

Two features of Figure 1 are worth noting. First, the proposed method exhibits a steep rise between weeks one and two, corresponding to the prototype on real data; the client can use something, badly, in the second week. Second, the baseline exhibits a local maximum at week 14 corresponding to delivery of the deck, followed by a decline as the deck is circulated. We conjecture, and Table 1 of [4] supports, that the asymptotic value of a slide deck satisfies

limn→∞ V(deckn) = 0,
(3)

where n is the slide count. We leave a formal proof to the reader, who has seen the deck.

Across engagements to date, code handover was achieved in 100 % of cases. No proprietary runtime, platform fee or licence was introduced. Systems built under the method were operated by the client's own engineers after handover, with Forward on call; the mean time between calls increased monotonically, which is the desired direction.

Limitations

Sample size. We are three people. The method does not scale and is not intended to. We will not sell you a bench of thirty juniors, because we do not have one and because that is the method we are proposing an alternative to. We take on a small number of clients at a time and say no to the rest, politely and quickly.

Applicability. The method fails when the workflow does not yet exist, when the data does not yet exist, or when the problem is a spreadsheet. In these cases we recommend, respectively, a process, a database and a spreadsheet, and we invoice for the week it took to find out. Roughly one engagement in three ends here. We regard this as the method working.

Validity of Figure 1. The baseline curve is reconstructed from the reports of clients and former colleagues and should be read as a caricature with a trend line. We are confident in the shape and would welcome a controlled comparison, which we expect to remain unfunded.

Dependencies. One author is married to a designer we need (Appendix A, Figure 6). This introduces a single point of failure in the visual layer of our deliverables, which we have chosen to accept rather than mitigate.

Model risk. Language models are non-deterministic, occasionally wrong with confidence, and change under one's feet when the provider ships an update. Section 3.2 exists because of this paragraph. We cannot remove the risk; we can measure it on every change and route the consequential cases to a person, and we do.

Ethics Statement

All systems built under the method process personal data belonging to the client's customers and employees and are therefore subject to the General Data Protection Regulation [5]. We treat this as an engineering constraint rather than a legal afterthought. Data minimisation is applied at the retrieval layer: a model is shown the fields the task requires and no others. Access is enforced under the permissions of the human on whose behalf the system acts, never under a service account with broader rights. Every model call and every tool invocation is logged for audit. Client data is not used to train, fine-tune or evaluate anything outside the client's own tenancy, and we sign a data processing agreement before we read a single record.

Where data must not leave the organisation or the Union, inference is run on-premise or on EU-resident infrastructure (Section 3.3). Where a system's output can affect a person—credit, employment, health, a customer's money—a human reviews the output before it takes effect, and the system is designed so that removing the human is a deliberate change that fails the evaluation suite rather than a configuration toggle.

We will tell a client when AI is the wrong tool, when their data is not fit for it, or when the proposed use would be lawful but unkind. We have done so and expect to again. No animals were harmed in the preparation of this work. One slide deck was.

Conclusion and Future Work

We conclude that the shortest path from an AI demonstration to an AI system in production is a senior engineer with access to the data, a deadline, and permission to say no. We are those engineers. We embed, we build on real data in the first fortnight, we harden for the remaining six weeks, and we leave behind code your team can run.

Future work proceeds in two directions. First, the components that recur across engagements—evaluation harnesses, connectors to the usual Nordic ERP and CRM systems, the human-review step—are candidates for productisation, and we intend to productise them slowly and only once they have stopped changing. Second, we are exploring purpose-built hardware for private and local inference, which our accountant has asked us to describe as exploratory and which we hereby do.
